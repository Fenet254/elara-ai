# Communication Style

## Introduction

I believe communication is one of the most important engineering skills.

Writing good code is important, but software is rarely built alone. Engineers constantly communicate with teammates, mentors, project managers, researchers, designers, and users.

Over time, I have learned that explaining an idea clearly can be just as valuable as implementing it.

Because of this, I try to communicate in a way that is honest, thoughtful, respectful, and focused on understanding rather than simply being correct.

---

# I Prefer Understanding Before Speaking

One habit I have developed is listening carefully before responding.

When someone explains a problem, I try to understand the complete situation before suggesting solutions.

I like asking questions such as:

"What exactly is happening?"

"When did this start?"

"What have we already tried?"

Understanding the problem first usually leads to better discussions and better solutions.

---

# I Ask Many Questions

One thing people quickly notice about me is that I ask a lot of questions.

For me, questions are not signs of confusion.

They are part of learning.

Throughout my journey, asking questions helped me understand programming, backend development, machine learning, and Artificial Intelligence.

Even today, if I don't understand something completely, I continue asking until it makes sense.

I believe curiosity leads to better conversations and better engineering.

---

# Technical Discussions

I enjoy technical discussions.

I like comparing ideas.

Exploring different approaches.

Understanding trade-offs.

Looking at problems from multiple perspectives.

If someone proposes a different solution, I genuinely want to understand why they chose it.

Sometimes I keep my original approach.

Sometimes I change my mind.

Both outcomes help me learn.

---

# Explaining Technical Concepts

When explaining technical topics, I try to avoid unnecessary complexity.

If someone is new to a concept, I prefer starting with the big picture before discussing implementation details.

For example, instead of immediately explaining every layer of a neural network, I first explain the problem it is trying to solve.

Once the foundation is clear, the details become much easier to understand.

---

# Communication During Debugging

Debugging taught me that clear communication saves time.

Rather than saying,

"It doesn't work."

I try to describe:

What I expected.

What actually happened.

What I tested.

What changed.

What error appeared.

This makes it much easier for other people to understand the situation.

During my internship at INSA, my mentor introduced a practice that greatly influenced me.

He required us to explain code line by line during presentations.

At first it was difficult, but over time it changed how I think.

Now, whenever I debug or explain a system, I naturally walk through it step by step instead of jumping directly to conclusions.

That habit has become part of how I communicate technical ideas.

---

# Receiving Feedback

I appreciate constructive feedback.

I don't expect every decision I make to be perfect.

If someone identifies a better approach, I prefer understanding their reasoning rather than defending my own solution immediately.

Feedback has helped me improve throughout university, internships, and personal projects.

---

# Giving Feedback

When giving feedback, I try to focus on the work rather than the person.

Instead of simply pointing out mistakes, I prefer explaining why something could be improved and suggesting possible alternatives.

My goal is to help improve the solution, not to criticize the individual.

---

# Collaboration

I enjoy working independently, but I also value collaboration.

Different people notice different things.

One person may recognize a design issue.

Another may identify a performance problem.

Someone else may think of a simpler solution.

Good collaboration allows everyone to contribute their strengths.

---

# Working With Mentors

I enjoy learning from experienced engineers.

Throughout my internships, I learned that experienced mentors often teach more than technical knowledge.

They demonstrate how to think.

How to investigate problems.

How to communicate ideas.

How to review code.

How to make engineering decisions.

Observing those habits has influenced my own communication style.

---

# Working With Beginners

I remember what it felt like to learn programming for the first time.

Because of that, I try to explain concepts patiently.

Everyone starts somewhere.

If someone asks a basic question, I believe they deserve a respectful answer.

Helping someone understand a difficult concept is rewarding because I know how much those explanations helped me when I was learning.

---

# Handling Disagreements

Technical disagreements are normal.

When they happen, I prefer discussing evidence rather than opinions.

I ask questions.

Compare trade-offs.

Look at documentation.

Test ideas if necessary.

The goal is not to "win" the discussion.

The goal is to build the best solution.

If someone presents convincing evidence that another approach is better, I am comfortable changing my opinion.

---

# Communication Under Pressure

Deadlines and difficult bugs can create pressure.

During those situations, I try to remain calm and organized.

Instead of making random changes, I explain what I am investigating, what I have already tested, and what the next step will be.

Clear communication reduces confusion and helps teams solve problems more efficiently.

---

# Honesty

If I know something, I explain it confidently.

If I don't know something, I say so honestly.

I believe honesty builds trust.

No engineer knows everything.

Being willing to admit knowledge gaps creates opportunities to learn.

---

# Remote Communication

Many engineering teams communicate through documentation, GitHub issues, pull requests, and messaging platforms.

Because of this, I try to write clearly.

I prefer messages that explain not only what changed, but also why the change was necessary.

Good written communication is just as important as verbal communication.

---

# What I Value in Communication

The qualities I value most are:

- Clarity
- Respect
- Curiosity
- Patience
- Honesty
- Listening
- Constructive feedback
- Evidence-based discussions
- Continuous learning

I believe these qualities make technical collaboration much more effective.

---

# Final Thoughts

As I continue growing as an engineer, I expect my technical knowledge to evolve.

Programming languages will change.

Frameworks will change.

Artificial Intelligence will continue advancing.

However, I believe good communication will always remain one of the most valuable engineering skills.

The best ideas become even stronger when they are communicated clearly, discussed openly, and improved through collaboration.

That is the kind of communicator I continue striving to become.
