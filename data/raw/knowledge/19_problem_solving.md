# Problem Solving

## Introduction

One of the reasons I enjoy software engineering is that every project is a collection of problems waiting to be solved.

Some problems are small and can be fixed in minutes.

Others require hours or even days of investigation.

Over time, I realized that becoming a better engineer is not about avoiding difficult problems. It is about learning how to approach them in a structured way.

Today, whenever I encounter a challenge, I try to understand it before trying to solve it.

That mindset has become one of the defining characteristics of how I work.

---

# My First Step Is Understanding the Problem

I rarely start writing code immediately.

Instead, I first try to understand what the real problem is.

Many technical issues appear complicated at first, but after breaking them down, they usually become much simpler.

Before making changes, I ask myself questions like:

• What exactly is happening?

• What should have happened instead?

• When did the problem begin?

• Can I reproduce it consistently?

• Is this the real problem, or only a symptom of another issue?

Taking a few minutes to understand the problem often saves hours of unnecessary work.

---

# Breaking Large Problems Into Smaller Ones

Large systems can feel overwhelming.

Instead of trying to understand everything at once, I divide the problem into smaller parts.

For example, if an AI application is not producing the expected output, I separate the workflow into stages.

Is the data correct?

Did preprocessing work?

Are the inputs correct?

Is the model behaving correctly?

Is inference working properly?

Is the output being interpreted correctly?

Once each stage is isolated, finding the issue becomes much easier.

---

# Root Cause Over Temporary Fixes

One principle guides almost every debugging session I have.

Fix the cause.

Not the symptom.

Sometimes it is possible to make an error disappear with a quick workaround.

But if I do not understand why it happened, the same issue will likely appear again later.

I prefer spending more time understanding the underlying cause because it usually prevents future problems as well.

---

# Debugging Is Investigation

I think of debugging as an investigation rather than trial and error.

Instead of changing random parts of the code and hoping something works, I collect information.

I read error messages carefully.

I compare expected and actual behavior.

I inspect variables.

I verify assumptions.

I simplify the problem whenever possible.

Every new piece of information reduces the number of possible causes.

Eventually the problem becomes small enough to solve confidently.

---

# Learning Line by Line

One habit that had a significant impact on my problem-solving approach came from my internship at INSA.

My mentor required every intern to explain code line by line during technical presentations.

At first it felt slow.

Later I realized why it was valuable.

Explaining every line forced me to understand not only what the code was doing but also why it was doing it.

That experience permanently changed how I debug software.

Today, whenever I investigate a problem, I naturally walk through the execution step by step instead of making assumptions.

This habit has helped me solve problems more systematically.

---

# I Try to Eliminate Assumptions

Assumptions often create bugs.

If I think I know what the system is doing without verifying it, I can spend hours investigating the wrong problem.

Instead, I prefer checking.

Print values.

Inspect logs.

Read documentation.

Verify configurations.

Confirm inputs and outputs.

Evidence is more valuable than assumptions.

---

# Research Before Reinventing

Not every problem requires inventing a completely new solution.

If I encounter something unfamiliar, I research it.

I read official documentation.

I compare different approaches.

I study how experienced engineers solved similar problems.

After understanding the available options, I decide which solution fits my situation best.

Learning from existing knowledge is part of good engineering.

---

# Practice Builds Better Decisions

Early in my programming journey, I often depended heavily on tutorials.

Over time, I realized that following tutorials teaches syntax, but solving problems independently teaches engineering.

Every personal project forced me to make decisions.

Choosing technologies.

Designing systems.

Debugging failures.

Improving performance.

Those experiences gradually built confidence.

Now I rely more on understanding than on step-by-step instructions.

---

# Learning From Failure

Not every experiment succeeds.

Some models perform poorly.

Some architectures fail.

Some ideas simply do not work.

Instead of seeing those moments as failures, I see them as feedback.

Every unsuccessful attempt teaches me something.

Sometimes it teaches me what to avoid.

Sometimes it reveals an incorrect assumption.

Sometimes it points toward a better solution.

Failure is part of the learning process.

---

# Making Technical Decisions

When several solutions are possible, I usually evaluate them using a few questions.

Is it correct?

Will it be easy to maintain?

Can another developer understand it?

Will it scale if the project grows?

Am I introducing unnecessary complexity?

Can I explain why I chose this approach?

If I cannot justify a decision, I continue investigating before committing to it.

---

# Handling Pressure

Difficult bugs and approaching deadlines are normal parts of software engineering.

When pressure increases, I try not to panic.

Instead, I slow down.

I organize my thoughts.

I write down what I already know.

I identify the next small step.

Solving one small problem at a time is usually more effective than trying to solve everything at once.

---

# Knowing When to Ask for Help

I enjoy solving problems independently, but I also recognize the value of asking for help.

If I have investigated a problem thoroughly and still cannot find the answer, discussing it with another engineer often provides a new perspective.

Sometimes simply explaining the problem out loud reveals something I previously overlooked.

Collaboration is part of effective problem solving.

---

# Every Problem Improves Me

One thing I appreciate about software engineering is that every challenge leaves me with more knowledge than I had before.

Each bug teaches me something.

Each project improves my judgment.

Each discussion expands my perspective.

Over time, those small lessons accumulate into experience.

That is why I enjoy solving difficult problems rather than avoiding them.

---

# My Problem-Solving Philosophy

If I had to summarize my approach in a few principles, they would be:

• Understand before changing.

• Verify instead of assuming.

• Divide large problems into smaller ones.

• Search for the root cause.

• Learn from every failure.

• Stay curious.

• Ask questions.

• Continue improving.

These principles have guided my work throughout university, personal projects, internships, and research.

I expect they will continue guiding me throughout my career.

---

# Final Thoughts

Problem solving is one of the reasons I chose software engineering.

Technology will continue changing.

Programming languages will evolve.

Artificial Intelligence will become more advanced.

New tools will appear.

But the ability to think carefully, investigate systematically, learn continuously, and solve difficult problems will always remain valuable.

That is the kind of engineer I continue working to become.
