# Failure and Lessons

## Introduction

Failure is an unavoidable part of learning engineering.

Every developer writes code that does not work the first time.

Every engineer encounters bugs they cannot immediately explain.

Every project introduces unexpected problems.

Rather than seeing failure as something negative, I try to see it as feedback.

Many of my biggest improvements have come from problems that initially seemed impossible to solve.

---

# Learning That Tutorials Are Only the Beginning

When I first started programming, I depended heavily on YouTube tutorials.

Tutorials helped me understand syntax and basic programming concepts.

However, I eventually realized something important.

Following a tutorial teaches you how someone else solves a problem.

It does not necessarily teach you how to solve new problems on your own.

The first time I built a project without copying a tutorial, I struggled much more than expected.

Simple decisions suddenly became difficult because there was no instructor telling me what to do next.

That experience taught me that true learning begins after the tutorial ends.

---

# The Calculator That Refused to Divide

One of my earliest projects was a calculator.

Most operations worked quickly.

Division did not.

I spent nearly two weeks trying different ideas.

I searched Google.

I watched more tutorials.

I tested different solutions.

For a while, it felt like I was making no progress.

Eventually I solved the problem.

Looking back, the technical difficulty was small compared to what I face today, but the lesson was significant.

Persistence often matters more than immediate success.

---

# Learning to Be Patient During Debugging

Earlier in my journey, I sometimes changed several parts of my code at once when something failed.

Sometimes the problem disappeared.

Sometimes it became even worse.

Over time I realized that changing multiple things at once makes debugging much harder.

Now I prefer changing one thing at a time.

That allows me to understand exactly what caused the issue instead of guessing.

This approach has saved me countless hours.

---

# Understanding Before Fixing

One lesson that changed the way I debug came from my internship at INSA.

During code presentations, our mentor encouraged us to explain programs line by line.

At first this felt slow.

Eventually I realized that slowing down actually helped me understand programs much more deeply.

Today I naturally trace execution step by step before attempting a fix.

Understanding comes before modification.

---

# Research Is Often Slow

As I became interested in artificial intelligence and machine learning, I discovered that progress is rarely immediate.

Training models takes time.

Experiments fail.

Different architectures produce different results.

Sometimes a model performs worse than expected even though everything appears correct.

Instead of assuming something is broken, I learned to investigate carefully.

Research taught me patience.

---

# Learning From Difficult Projects

Larger projects introduced challenges that smaller applications never exposed.

Managing project structure.

Keeping code organized.

Maintaining consistency.

Debugging interactions between different components.

Thinking about scalability.

These experiences taught me that software engineering is much more than writing code.

Planning and organization are just as important.

---

# Accepting That I Do Not Know Everything

One important lesson I continue to learn is that no engineer knows everything.

Technology changes constantly.

New frameworks appear.

Better techniques are developed.

Rather than pretending to know everything, I try to stay curious.

I ask questions.

I read documentation.

I learn from mentors and other engineers.

Remaining teachable is one of the most valuable qualities an engineer can have.

---

# Mistakes Improve Future Decisions

Whenever I solve a difficult problem, I try to understand why it happened.

Instead of simply fixing an issue, I ask questions such as:

- What caused this problem?
- Could it have been prevented?
- How can I avoid making the same mistake again?
- What did this experience teach me?

Reflecting on mistakes helps me improve future decisions.

---

# Lessons From Internships

Working alongside experienced engineers taught me that software development is an iterative process.

Receiving constructive feedback is part of becoming a better engineer.

Code reviews.

Technical discussions.

Presentations.

Mentorship.

These experiences helped me realize that improving software often means improving communication and collaboration as well.

---

# Failure Builds Confidence

One interesting realization is that solving difficult problems increases confidence far more than solving easy ones.

Every challenge that initially seemed impossible eventually became another lesson.

Those experiences remind me that difficult problems are temporary.

With enough patience, careful thinking, and persistence, progress is almost always possible.

---

# Lessons That Continue to Guide Me

Some of the most important lessons I have learned include:

- Practice is more valuable than memorization.
- Understanding is more important than copying solutions.
- Tutorials are starting points, not destinations.
- Debugging requires patience.
- Research takes time.
- Asking questions accelerates learning.
- Small improvements accumulate over time.
- Every mistake contains a lesson.
- Consistency is more important than speed.
- Learning never truly ends.

---

# Final Thoughts

Looking back, I do not regret the mistakes I made.

Without them, I would not have developed the habits and mindset that I rely on today.

Every failed experiment, every debugging session, and every difficult project has contributed to my growth.

Failure is not the opposite of success.

For me, failure is one of the ways success is built.
