# Daily Workflow

## Introduction

Every engineer eventually develops a workflow that fits the way they think.

My workflow has changed over the years as I gained more experience through university projects, internships, research, and personal projects.

Today I focus less on writing code as quickly as possible and more on understanding problems, planning carefully, and improving continuously.

Although every project is different, my overall process remains fairly consistent.

---

# Understanding Before Coding

I rarely start writing code immediately.

Instead, I first try to understand the problem completely.

I ask myself questions such as:

- What exactly am I trying to build?
- Who will use it?
- What inputs and outputs should it have?
- What technologies make the most sense?
- What could become difficult later?

Spending time thinking before coding usually saves much more time later.

---

# Research First

If I encounter an unfamiliar technology, library, or concept, I research it before implementing anything.

This usually includes:

- Reading official documentation.
- Searching technical articles.
- Watching demonstrations when appropriate.
- Looking at open-source implementations.
- Comparing different approaches.

I prefer understanding why something works instead of simply copying code.

---

# Planning the Project

Before implementation, I usually divide a project into smaller tasks.

Instead of thinking about the entire system at once, I break it into manageable components.

For example:

- User interface
- Backend API
- Database
- Authentication
- Machine learning model
- Testing
- Deployment

Breaking projects into smaller parts makes them much easier to manage.

---

# Building Incrementally

I prefer making gradual progress instead of trying to finish everything in one sitting.

My workflow is usually:

- Build one feature.
- Test it.
- Fix problems.
- Improve the implementation.
- Move to the next feature.

Small improvements are easier to understand and easier to debug.

---

# Debugging as Part of Development

Debugging is not something I do only after problems appear.

It is part of my development process.

I frequently test my work while building.

If something behaves unexpectedly, I investigate immediately rather than allowing small issues to accumulate.

This reduces larger problems later.

---

# My Line-by-Line Debugging Habit

One habit that has had a significant impact on my workflow came from my internship at INSA.

Our mentor asked us to explain programs line by line during technical presentations.

At first this seemed like a presentation exercise.

Eventually it became one of the most valuable debugging techniques I have learned.

Now I naturally trace execution step by step whenever something behaves unexpectedly.

Instead of guessing, I follow the program's execution carefully until I understand the root cause.

This habit continues to influence how I solve problems today.

---

# Learning While Building

One principle I strongly believe is that projects are one of the best teachers.

Whenever I build something new, I almost always encounter concepts I have never seen before.

Instead of avoiding those challenges, I treat them as opportunities to learn.

Every project expands my knowledge beyond what tutorials alone can provide.

---

# Documentation and Notes

Whenever I discover something useful, I try to document it.

This might include:

- Commands.
- Configuration steps.
- Common errors.
- Debugging techniques.
- Important concepts.

Writing things down helps reinforce my understanding and makes future work easier.

---

# Continuous Improvement

After finishing a project, I rarely consider it completely finished.

I usually think about questions such as:

- Could this architecture be improved?
- Is the code easier to read now?
- Can performance be improved?
- Can duplication be reduced?
- Is there a cleaner solution?

Continuous improvement has become part of my engineering mindset.

---

# Learning Every Day

Technology evolves quickly.

Because of that, I try to learn something regularly.

Sometimes that means:

- Reading documentation.
- Experimenting with a new framework.
- Exploring research papers.
- Watching conference talks.
- Building a small prototype.
- Improving an existing project.

Learning has become part of my daily routine rather than a separate activity.

---

# Collaboration

Although I enjoy building independently, I also value collaboration.

Working with mentors, instructors, and other engineers has shown me that discussing ideas often leads to better solutions.

Different perspectives help identify problems that one person might overlook.

---

# Balancing Speed and Quality

I do not believe that writing code quickly is always the goal.

Sometimes moving more slowly produces a better result.

I try to balance:

- Speed
- Accuracy
- Maintainability
- Simplicity
- Readability

A solution that is easy to understand is usually easier to maintain in the future.

---

# Typical Workflow

A typical project often follows this pattern:

1. Understand the problem.
2. Research unfamiliar concepts.
3. Plan the architecture.
4. Break the work into smaller tasks.
5. Build incrementally.
6. Test continuously.
7. Debug carefully.
8. Improve the implementation.
9. Document important lessons.
10. Reflect on what I learned.

---

# Core Principles

The principles that guide my daily workflow include:

- Understand before implementing.
- Research before assuming.
- Build incrementally.
- Test continuously.
- Debug systematically.
- Learn from every project.
- Document useful knowledge.
- Improve continuously.
- Stay curious.

---

# Final Thoughts

My workflow continues to evolve as I gain more experience.

Every project teaches me new habits, new techniques, and better ways of thinking.

Rather than trying to find a perfect workflow, I focus on continuously improving the way I learn, build, debug, and collaborate.

That mindset has become one of the most important parts of my growth as a software engineer.
