# Learning Strategy

## Introduction

One of the things that has shaped my journey the most is curiosity.

I rarely learn a technology simply because it is popular.

Most of the time, I start learning because I have a question that I genuinely want answered.

Questions like:

- How do websites remember users after logging in?
- How does ChatGPT understand language?
- How does a neural network actually learn?
- How do recommendation systems work?
- Why is one algorithm better than another?

Those questions become projects.

Those projects become experience.

That experience becomes knowledge.

---

# Curiosity Is My Starting Point

Most of my learning begins with curiosity rather than obligation.

If I use a website, application, or AI tool and something interests me, I naturally begin asking questions about how it works.

Instead of simply using technology, I enjoy understanding what happens behind the scenes.

That curiosity has guided almost every stage of my learning journey.

---

# Learning by Building

I believe projects teach more than tutorials.

Reading documentation explains concepts.

Watching videos explains ideas.

Building something forces understanding.

Whenever I learn a new technology, I try to build a project with it as soon as possible.

Building reveals problems that tutorials never show.

It forces me to think independently and solve real engineering challenges.

---

# Breaking Down Difficult Topics

Large topics can feel overwhelming.

Instead of trying to learn everything at once, I divide concepts into smaller pieces.

For example, if I want to learn a new framework, I might approach it like this:

1. Understand the basic concepts.
2. Build a small example.
3. Learn project structure.
4. Add more features.
5. Read official documentation.
6. Improve the project.

Learning step by step makes difficult subjects much easier to understand.

---

# Documentation Before Shortcuts

Whenever possible, I prefer reading official documentation.

Documentation explains not only how something works but also why it was designed that way.

Although videos and blogs are helpful, documentation is usually my most trusted source.

Learning how to read documentation has become one of my most valuable engineering skills.

---

# Practice Creates Confidence

One lesson I learned early is that practice matters more than memorization.

Watching someone solve a problem is useful.

Solving it myself is far more valuable.

Many concepts only became clear after I implemented them several times.

Confidence grows through repetition.

---

# Learning From Mistakes

Mistakes are part of my learning process.

Whenever something fails, I ask questions like:

- Why did this happen?
- What assumption was incorrect?
- How could I prevent this in the future?

Instead of becoming discouraged by mistakes, I try to treat them as opportunities to improve.

Some of my biggest lessons came from debugging problems that initially seemed impossible.

---

# Research Before Asking

When I encounter a problem, I usually try to investigate it myself first.

My typical process is:

- Read the error carefully.
- Search official documentation.
- Search technical discussions.
- Experiment with different approaches.
- Test small examples.

If I still cannot understand the problem, I seek help from mentors or experienced developers.

This approach helps me become more independent while still learning from others.

---

# Learning From Mentors

One of the biggest influences on my learning came during my internship at INSA.

Our mentor encouraged us to explain code line by line during technical presentations.

That habit completely changed how I debug and understand software.

Instead of jumping directly to solutions, I learned to slow down and understand execution flow.

I continue using that strategy today.

---

# Learning Through Teaching

I have noticed that explaining a concept often reveals how well I actually understand it.

If I cannot explain something clearly, it usually means I need to study it more deeply.

Teaching has become another way for me to strengthen my own understanding.

---

# Continuous Improvement

Technology changes constantly.

Because of that, I never consider my education complete.

There is always another framework to explore.

Another research paper to read.

Another project to build.

Another problem to solve.

Rather than trying to know everything, I focus on becoming better every day.

---

# My Learning Routine

Although every week is different, my learning process usually looks like this:

- Learn a concept.
- Read documentation.
- Build a small project.
- Make mistakes.
- Debug carefully.
- Improve the implementation.
- Reflect on what I learned.
- Apply that knowledge in future projects.

Repeating this cycle has helped me grow consistently.

---

# Learning New Technologies

When learning something completely unfamiliar, I avoid trying to master everything immediately.

Instead, I focus on understanding the fundamentals first.

Once I understand the core ideas, learning advanced topics becomes much easier.

Strong foundations make future learning faster.

---

# My Learning Principles

The principles that guide my learning include:

- Stay curious.
- Ask questions.
- Build real projects.
- Practice consistently.
- Read documentation.
- Learn from mentors.
- Learn from mistakes.
- Understand before memorizing.
- Improve continuously.
- Never stop learning.

---

# Looking Forward

As technology continues evolving, I expect my learning strategy to evolve as well.

New programming languages, AI models, research areas, and engineering practices will continue to appear.

Rather than seeing that as overwhelming, I see it as exciting.

One of the reasons I enjoy software engineering is that there is always something new to discover.

That constant opportunity to learn is one of the biggest reasons I chose this field.

---

# Final Thoughts

If I had to summarize my learning strategy in one sentence, it would be this:

**Curiosity starts the journey, projects build understanding, mistakes create experience, and consistency leads to growth.**

That philosophy has guided my journey from writing my first "Hello, World!" program to working on AI research, internships, and increasingly complex engineering projects.

I believe it will continue guiding my learning throughout my career.
