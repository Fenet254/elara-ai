# Mentor Lessons

## Introduction

No engineer grows entirely alone.

While curiosity, practice, and persistence have driven much of my learning, mentors have played an important role in helping me develop better habits and better ways of thinking.

One thing I have learned is that mentors do not simply provide answers.

The best mentors teach you how to think.

They encourage you to ask better questions, understand problems more deeply, and become capable of solving future challenges independently.

The lessons in this document continue to influence how I approach software engineering today.

---

# Learning to Understand Before Explaining

One of the most valuable lessons I learned came during my internship at INSA.

Our mentor encouraged us to present our code line by line.

Instead of only showing the final result, we had to explain what every important section of the program was doing.

At first, I viewed this as a presentation exercise.

Over time, I realized it completely changed the way I approached programming.

Rather than jumping directly to a solution, I learned to understand exactly how the program executed.

Today, that habit has become one of the foundations of my debugging process.

Whenever I encounter a difficult problem, I naturally follow the execution step by step until I understand the root cause.

---

# Never Guess

Another lesson I learned is that assumptions often create more problems.

If I am unsure why something behaves a certain way, I try not to guess.

Instead, I investigate.

I examine the code.

I trace execution.

I read documentation.

I reproduce the problem.

Only after understanding the cause do I begin making changes.

This approach has made debugging much more systematic.

---

# Ask Better Questions

Good mentors rarely answer every question immediately.

Instead, they encourage better thinking.

One habit I have developed is spending time understanding a problem before asking for help.

When I do ask questions, I try to be specific.

Instead of asking:

"My project doesn't work."

I prefer questions like:

"I expected this function to return this result, but instead it returns this. Could the issue be related to this section of the code?"

Learning to ask better questions often leads to better answers.

---

# Documentation Is a Mentor

Although people are valuable teachers, I also think official documentation acts as another form of mentorship.

Documentation reflects the knowledge of the engineers who built a technology.

Reading documentation has taught me not only how tools work, but why they were designed the way they are.

Because of this, documentation has become one of the first places I look when learning something new.

---

# Feedback Is an Opportunity

Receiving feedback can sometimes be uncomfortable.

However, I have learned that constructive feedback is one of the fastest ways to improve.

Whether the feedback comes from a mentor, an instructor, or a teammate, I try to understand the reasoning behind it.

Good feedback helps reveal mistakes that I might not notice on my own.

---

# Learning From Observation

Not every lesson is taught directly.

Sometimes simply watching experienced engineers solve problems teaches valuable habits.

I pay attention to how experienced developers:

- Read code.
- Investigate bugs.
- Organize projects.
- Explain technical ideas.
- Break large problems into smaller ones.

These observations have gradually influenced my own workflow.

---

# Patience Is Part of Engineering

One lesson I continue to appreciate is that good engineering cannot always be rushed.

Complex problems often require careful investigation.

Trying to move too quickly usually creates additional mistakes.

Taking time to understand the problem almost always produces better solutions.

---

# Professionalism Matters

Technical ability is important.

Professional behavior is equally important.

My internships taught me that engineers are expected to communicate clearly, document their work, explain their decisions, and collaborate effectively.

Professional engineering involves much more than writing code.

---

# Learning Never Stops

Every mentor I have learned from demonstrates one important quality.

They continue learning.

Even experienced engineers read documentation, explore new technologies, and experiment with different approaches.

Watching this reinforced my belief that software engineering is a career of continuous learning.

There is always another concept to explore and another skill to improve.

---

# Becoming Independent

A mentor's goal is not to make students dependent.

It is to help them become independent thinkers.

As I continue growing, I try to rely less on memorized solutions and more on understanding.

The more I understand the reasoning behind a solution, the more prepared I become for unfamiliar challenges.

---

# Lessons That Stay With Me

Some of the lessons that continue to guide me include:

- Understand before changing code.
- Debug systematically rather than guessing.
- Read documentation carefully.
- Ask thoughtful questions.
- Accept constructive feedback.
- Learn by observing experienced engineers.
- Stay patient during difficult problems.
- Continue learning throughout your career.
- Build understanding rather than memorization.

---

# My Goal Moving Forward

As I gain more experience, I hope to become the kind of engineer who can mentor others.

Many people have invested time in helping me grow.

In the future, I would like to pass those lessons on by helping students, collaborating with teammates, contributing to open-source communities, and sharing knowledge with other engineers.

Teaching others is one of the best ways to strengthen both the engineering community and my own understanding.

---

# Final Thoughts

When I think about mentorship, I do not only think about learning technical skills.

I think about learning better habits.

Learning better ways to think.

Learning how to approach problems with patience and curiosity.

The lessons I have received from mentors continue to shape how I learn, how I debug, and how I approach engineering every day.

I expect those lessons to remain part of my career for many years to come.
